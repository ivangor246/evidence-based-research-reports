# Business and market research

Use for business ideas, validation, markets, competitors, business models, and commercial opportunities.

## Find and diversify ideas
1. Define geography, buyer type, resources, launch horizon, and business model. State assumptions if missing.
2. Map distinct buyer segments and workflows. Find recurring, costly problems across several segments when the scope allows.
3. Describe each idea as buyer → problem → current alternative → proposed value.
4. Group near-duplicates. Do not count minor feature variations as separate ideas. Aim for a varied set of sectors, buyers, and problems unless the user specifies a narrow market.
5. For a large list, summarize all ideas, shortlist a manageable group, and analyze the strongest few in depth.

## Separate evidence levels
Report the strongest level reached for each idea:
1. **Problem exists:** cases, complaints, workarounds, or documentation.
2. **Problem repeats:** frequency or recurring cost is evidenced.
3. **Commercial interest:** target buyers express interest, have budget, or evaluate a paid solution.
4. **Payment proof:** paid pilots, purchases, or renewals.

Competitor products and published prices show that an offer exists; they do not prove demand for this idea. Documentation proves a workflow or capability, not market size or willingness to pay.

## Compare ideas
Use a structured comparison suited to the decision. Qualitative comparison is valid; a numeric score is optional.

The following weights are an example, not a default requirement:

| Criterion | Example weight |
|---|---:|
| Problem evidence | 20% |
| Commercial validation | 20% |
| Buyer reachability | 15% |
| Differentiation | 15% |
| Repeat value | 15% |
| Feasibility and economics | 15% |

Adapt criteria and weights to the question, stage, sector, and available evidence. Explain material changes. Score only when the evidence supports meaningful comparison. Mark missing evidence as unknown; do not silently assign a neutral score. A provisional ranking is allowed if uncertainty is visible. Scores compare options; they are not probabilities of success.

## Competitors and alternatives
Separate:
- direct competitors;
- indirect or adjacent products;
- substitutes and manual workarounds;
- built-in or free platform features;
- internal solutions developed by the buyer.

Compare the same fields: target buyer, problem, coverage, key functions, price and date, onboarding, integrations, limits, geography, and adoption evidence when public. Distinguish vendor claims from independent reviews. Do not claim "no competitors" from a limited search.

## Acquisition channels
Do not default to search advertising. Consider channels that fit the buyer: search, communities, marketplaces, platform directories, integrators, partners, referrals, outbound, industry events, and existing customer networks. When go-to-market is in scope, assess relevant channels by buyer intent, reach, evidence, likely cost, and access barriers; propose a small test only when useful. Search-result presence is not search volume; query intent is not demand. Mark unavailable metrics as unknown.

## Validation and economics
For a new idea or commercial hypothesis, test problem frequency, workaround or spend, buyer, alternatives, willingness to pay, repeat use, and onboarding. Design low-cost tests with target participants, observable success criteria, budget, and stop rules. Do not impose experiments or willingness-to-pay tests on general market or competitor analysis unless requested or needed to answer the question.

When launch feasibility or unit economics are in scope, separate build costs, fixed monthly costs, variable cost per customer, acquisition, support, taxes, payment fees, and reserve. Show formulas, ranges, and assumptions. Where data allow, calculate contribution per customer, customer count to cover fixed costs, acquisition cost, and payback period. Label untested prices as test prices. Break-even must include all relevant costs; do not call partial budget coverage business break-even.

Estimate market size when relevant and only with defensible buyer counts and price assumptions. Never infer payment demand from general industry size.

## Conclusion
For idea validation, use calibrated outcomes such as worth testing, evidence incomplete, or significant barriers. For other business research, answer the stated question and present only relevant findings, risks, and limitations.
